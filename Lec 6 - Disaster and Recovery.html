01

What is a disaster

In an information security context, a disaster is any event that interrupts normal operations or damages information assets badly enough that recovery needs a planned, structured response rather than a routine fix. Where the assurance lecture was mostly about confidentiality and integrity, this topic is mainly a fight to protect availability, the third pillar of the CIA triad.

Where disasters come from

Natural
Environmental

Floods, fires, earthquakes, storms, anything driven by the physical world.

Technical
Infrastructure

Hardware failure, power loss, software corruption, network outages.

Human
Caused

Ransomware, sabotage, accidental deletion, a bad configuration pushed live.

The one-line version: a single point of failure becomes a disaster only once a trigger actually reaches it. Recovery planning exists to break that meeting, by removing the weak point, absorbing the trigger, or restoring service before the outage does real damage.
02

Contingency planning, the umbrella

Before splitting into BCP and DRP, it helps to meet the term that sits above both. A contingency plan is the organisation's overall set of documented procedures for responding to a system or business disruption, restoring operations, and managing damage during and after the event. NIST's guidance, SP 800-34, treats it as the umbrella that several more specific plans sit under.

Information System Contingency Plan family, NIST SP 800-34
Business Continuity Plankeeps the business running
Disaster Recovery Planrestores IT after a disaster
Continuity of Operations Plankeeps essential functions alive at an alternate site
Crisis Communication Planwhat gets said, to whom, and when
Cyber Incident Response Planhandles the security incident itself
Occupant Emergency Planprotects people and the physical site

A contingency plan is not one document, it is this whole family, each plan answering a narrower question so that nothing falls through the cracks between "keep the business alive" and "get this one server back online."

Why the umbrella matters for an exam question: if you are ever asked to place BCP, DRP, or incident response in relation to each other, contingency planning is the correct top level term, the others are its members, not synonyms for it.
03

Business continuity and disaster recovery

The two members of that family you will use most often. They get used almost interchangeably outside class, but they answer different questions.

BCP
Business Continuity Plan

Asks how the organisation keeps operating, even in a degraded state, while the disruption is happening. Covers people, alternate locations, and communication, not just computers.

DRP
Disaster Recovery Plan

The technical subset of that plan. Asks how IT systems, data, and infrastructure get restored to an operational state after the disruption.

BCP keeps the business alive · DRP brings the technology back to life
QuestionBCPDRP
ScopeWhole business, all departmentsIT systems and data specifically
TriggerAny disruption to normal operationsLoss or damage to IT infrastructure
Typical ownerExecutive or operations teamIT and security team
OutputAlternate processes, staffing, communication plansBackup, failover, and restoration procedures
04

RTO and RPO, the two numbers that drive everything

Every recovery plan is really built around two targets, and once you can define both from memory, most disaster recovery questions become easy.

TermQuestion it answersExample
RTO, Recovery Time ObjectiveHow long can the system stay down before the damage is unacceptableOnline banking must be back within 2 hours
RPO, Recovery Point ObjectiveHow much data can we afford to lose, measured in timeBackups every 15 minutes, so at most 15 minutes of data is lost

A simplification. Real organisations set RTO and RPO per system through a formal Business Impact Analysis, not a single slider.

A shorter RTO or RPO always costs more. Near zero data loss and near instant recovery means real time replication and a standby system running around the clock. Choosing these numbers is a business decision as much as a technical one.
05

Recovery site strategies

Once a disaster takes down the primary site, the organisation needs somewhere else to run from. The three classic options trade cost against how fast they can take over.

Cold site
25%

Empty facility, power and space only. Cheapest, slowest, days to weeks.

Warm site
60%

Hardware and connectivity ready, data still needs loading. Hours.

Hot site
95%

Fully mirrored, always in sync. Most expensive, minutes to take over.

Rings show readiness to take over, not cost. Cost runs the same direction, cold is cheapest, hot is the most expensive to maintain.

06

Backup strategies

Backups are the raw material recovery depends on. There is no disaster recovery plan without a backup that actually works, and actually gets tested.

StrategySpeed to createSpeed to restoreStorage cost
FullSlowFast, one file setHigh
IncrementalFastSlow, replay every incrementLow
DifferentialMediumMedium, full plus one fileMedium
Mirroring / replicationContinuousNear instantHighest
A backup that has never been restored and tested is not a real backup, it is a guess. Recovery testing belongs on the calendar, not just in the document. Backups also need their own security, an unencrypted backup is a confidentiality risk in its own right.

08

The contingency planning process

NIST SP 800-34 lays out the process behind every plan in the family from Section 2, in seven steps. Like assurance, this is a loop you revisit, not a document you file away.

1

Develop the contingency planning policy

Set the authority, scope, and resources for planning before writing any procedure.

2

Conduct the Business Impact Analysis

Identify critical systems, and assign each one an RTO and RPO based on what losing it would cost.

3

Identify preventive controls

Reduce the odds and severity of a disruption in the first place, redundancy, surge protection, patching.

4

Create contingency strategies

Choose backup methods and recovery site tiers that meet the targets from step 2.

5

Develop the contingency plan

Write the actual procedures, roles, and contacts needed to execute the strategy.

6

Test, train, and exercise

Run drills and full failover tests. This is where an untested plan gets caught before a real event does.

7

Maintain the plan

Update it as systems, staff, and threats change. A stale plan fails quietly, feeds back into step 1.

09

Worked example: a ransomware outage

Let's run one realistic scenario through every stage, start to finish.

A
ASSET

The patient records system at a regional clinic, running on a single on-premise database server.

S
SINGLE POINT OF FAILURE

No offsite copy of the database, all backups live on a drive attached to the same network.

D
DISASTER TRIGGER

A phishing email delivers ransomware that spreads overnight, encrypting the database and the attached backup drive alike.

O
OUTAGE

Staff cannot access patient records the next morning. Appointments are cancelled, clinical availability is gone.

I
IMPACT

Days of lost service, breach notification obligations, and a ransom demand nobody should pay, since paying does not guarantee the data comes back.

C
RECOVERY CONTROL

An offsite, offline backup the ransomware could never reach, restored to a warm site, would have brought the clinic back within hours instead of days.

Notice this is the chain from Section 7 walked end to end on a real system, and the fix at the bottom closes the gap at the earliest possible link.
10

Quick self-test

Try to answer before you open each one.

Q1. A company sets an RPO of 1 hour for its sales database. What does this actually commit them to?

Backing up, or replicating, that database at least every hour, so at most one hour of transactions is ever lost. It says nothing about how fast the system comes back online, that is RTO's job.

Q2. Where does a Cyber Incident Response Plan sit relative to a contingency plan?

It is one member of the contingency plan family, focused specifically on detecting and handling the security incident itself, alongside BCP, DRP, and the others. Contingency planning is the umbrella term, not a synonym for any one of them.

Q3. Why might a company choose a warm site over a hot site, even for a fairly important system?

Cost. A hot site needs a fully mirrored environment running continuously. If the system can tolerate a few hours of downtime, a warm site delivers most of the resilience for a fraction of the cost.

Q4. A team keeps nightly full backups but has never tried restoring from one. What is wrong here?

An untested backup is only a guess. Corruption or a broken restore process is only discovered during a test, and finding out during a real disaster is far too late.

Q5. Two clinics have identical backup schedules. Clinic A stores backups on a drive attached to the production network. Clinic B keeps its backups offline and offsite. Same risk from ransomware?

No. Ransomware that spreads across the network can encrypt Clinic A's attached backup along with production, leaving nothing to restore from. Clinic B's offline, offsite copy is unreachable by the same attack, which is why isolation counts as a control in its own right.

11

Recap & glossary

Take this away

  • Disaster and recovery planning exists mainly to protect availability, the pillar assurance alone does not guarantee.
  • A contingency plan is the umbrella, BCP, DRP, COOP, crisis communication, incident response, and occupant emergency plans are its members.
  • RTO is about time to restore, RPO is about how much data you can afford to lose, both drive cost.
  • Recovery sites trade cost against speed, cold is cheap and slow, hot is expensive and near instant.
  • A backup that has never been restored and tested is not a real backup, it is a guess.
  • Planning is a loop, policy, BIA, controls, strategy, plan, test, maintain, repeat.

Glossary

Disaster
An event that interrupts normal operations or damages information assets badly enough to require a planned recovery response.
Contingency plan
The umbrella term for an organisation's documented procedures for responding to and recovering from a disruption.
Business Continuity Plan, BCP
The organisation wide plan for continuing operations during and after a disruption.
Disaster Recovery Plan, DRP
The technical plan for restoring IT systems and data after a disruption.
Continuity of Operations Plan, COOP
Keeps an organisation's essential functions running from an alternate location.
Recovery Time Objective, RTO
The maximum acceptable length of downtime before the damage becomes unacceptable.
Recovery Point Objective, RPO
The maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured as a length of time.
Cold, warm, hot site
Backup facilities ranging from empty space to a fully mirrored, always-synced environment.
Single point of failure, SPOF
Any component whose failure alone can bring down the whole system.
Business Impact Analysis, BIA
The process of identifying critical systems and setting their RTO and RPO based on the cost of losing them.
