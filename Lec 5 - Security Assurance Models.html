01

The big four

In Lecture 2 you met the words asset, vulnerability, threat and risk briefly. Today we slow down on four of them - Vulnerability, Threat, Attack and Countermeasure - because almost every security decision you will ever make is really just an argument about these four things and how they connect.

Start with something you already understand perfectly: your own house.

Formal definitions

TermPlain meaningHouse example
VulnerabilityA weakness that could be exploitedThe window latch doesn't fully close
ThreatA potential source of harm - something that could happenA burglar operating in the area
AttackThe actual attempt to exploit the weaknessThe burglar climbing in through that window
CountermeasureSomething that reduces the weakness, blocks the threat, or limits the damageA deadbolt, an alarm, a motion light
The one-line version: a threat becomes an attack only when it meets a vulnerability it can reach. Countermeasures exist to break that meeting - by removing the vulnerability, blocking the threat, or catching the attack before it does damage.
02

Vulnerability

A vulnerability is a weakness in a system, process, or person that could be exploited to violate confidentiality, integrity, or availability. On its own, a vulnerability does no harm - it's just a flaw waiting for something to find it.

Where vulnerabilities come from

Design

The blueprint itself is flawed - e.g. a protocol that sends passwords in plain text by design.

Implementation

The design was fine, the code wasn't - e.g. a buffer overflow bug, a missing input check.

Configuration

The software is fine, the setup isn't - e.g. default admin password left unchanged, an open port.

Human

People are the weak link - e.g. susceptibility to phishing, writing passwords on sticky notes.

Process

No procedure exists to catch the problem - e.g. no patching schedule, no offboarding checklist.

Physical

The real world is exploitable too - e.g. an unlocked server room, an unattended terminal.

Spot the category

Click each scenario to reveal which kind of vulnerability it is.

Vulnerability ≠ risk. A flaw that nobody can reach and nobody knows about carries very little real-world danger yet. It becomes dangerous only once a threat can reach it - which is exactly why the two terms are kept separate.

In the wild, publicly known vulnerabilities are catalogued as CVEs (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) and scored for severity with the CVSS scale - you'll see both again when we cover vulnerability management.

03

Threat

A threat is anything that has the potential to cause harm to an asset by exploiting a vulnerability. A threat is not yet an event - it's a standing possibility, made of a source, a capability, and (usually) an intent.

threat = threat agent (who/what) + capability (can they?) + intent (do they want to?) + opportunity (can they reach it?)

Where threats come from

Threat sourceNatureExample
Malicious humanIntentionalExternal hacker, cybercriminal, malicious insider, hacktivist, nation-state actor
Non-malicious humanUnintentionalCareless employee, untrained contractor, someone who mis-configures a server
Technical / environmentalNon-human, non-intentionalHardware failure, power outage, software bug triggering on its own
NaturalNon-human, non-intentionalFire, flood, earthquake damaging the data center

Rule of thumb: if you can picture it as "someone/something out there that could do X," it's a threat. If you can picture it as "a hole in our defenses," it's a vulnerability. If you're weighing how bad it would be and how likely it is, that's risk - see Section 7.
04

Attack

An attack is a threat put into action - a deliberate attempt to exploit a vulnerability and violate confidentiality, integrity, or availability. This is the only one of the four that is an event, not a standing condition.

Passive vs. active attacks

Passive attack

The attacker observes or copies data without altering it - eavesdropping, traffic analysis, sniffing an unencrypted connection. Hard to detect, because nothing changes; easier to prevent, usually with encryption.

Active attack

The attacker changes data, systems, or availability - modification, fabrication, interruption, replay, denial of service. Easier to detect, because something breaks or changes; harder to fully prevent.

You met the four classic attack effects in Lecture 1 - they map neatly onto the CIA triad:

EffectWhat happensCIA property hit
InterceptionAn unauthorized party gains access to data (passive)Confidentiality
InterruptionAn asset is destroyed or made unavailable (active)Availability
ModificationData is tampered with in transit or at rest (active)Integrity
FabricationFake data or identity is inserted into the system (active)Integrity / Authenticity

Attack surface and attack vector

Two terms you'll hear constantly: the attack surface is the total set of points where an attacker could try to get in (every open port, every input field, every employee inbox). An attack vector is the specific path actually used for one attack (a phishing email, an unpatched VPN, a stolen password).

05

Countermeasure

A countermeasure (or control) is any action, device, procedure, or technique that reduces a vulnerability, blocks a threat, or limits the damage of an attack. In Lecture 4 you met the actual mechanisms - gates, guards, cryptography - that implement countermeasures. Here we classify them by the job they do.

Five functional types

Preventive

Stops the attack before it happens. Locks, firewalls, MFA.

Deterrent

Discourages an attempt. Warning signs, visible cameras.

Detective

Notices an attack in progress or after the fact. IDS, audit logs.

Corrective

Fixes things once damage is found. Patches, restoring backups.

Compensating

An alternative safeguard when the ideal one isn't possible. E.g. extra monitoring on a system too old to patch.

Notice the pattern: preventive and deterrent controls act before an attack; detective controls act during; corrective and compensating controls act after. A mature security program needs all three time windows covered - prevention alone always eventually fails.

LinkWhat a shield there meansExample countermeasure
Threat → VulnerabilityKeep the threat from ever reaching the weaknessNetwork segmentation, access control, firewalls
Vulnerability → AttackRemove the weakness itself, so there's nothing to exploitPatching, secure coding, configuration hardening

Two ways to have zero risk: no vulnerability (nothing to exploit, even with threats circling) or no threat (nobody trying, even with holes wide open). In practice you rarely get either for free - which is why real security programs stack shields across all three links rather than betting on one.
07

Risk: putting a number on it

Risk is what you get once you combine how likely an attack is with how bad it would be. It's the quantity that actually drives decisions - you can't defend against everything, so you defend against the highest risk first.

Risk ≈ Likelihood ( Threat level × Vulnerability severity ) × Impact

Why bother? No organisation can patch every vulnerability or watch every threat with equal urgency. Turning threat, vulnerability and impact into a comparable risk score is what lets a security team say "fix this one first" with a straight face.
08

The assurance lifecycle

Security assurance is the confidence that a system actually meets its security requirements - and confidence isn't a one-time checkbox, it's a loop you run continuously as threats, vulnerabilities and systems all keep changing.

StageQuestion it answersFeeds into
IdentifyWhat do we have, and who might want to harm it?Asset inventory + threat list
AssessWhere are we weak, and how bad would it be?Vulnerability scan + risk rating
MitigateWhat do we do about the risks we can't accept?Countermeasures deployed
MonitorIs it working, and has anything changed?Logs, alerts - feeds back into Identify

This loop is the shared skeleton behind formal frameworks you'll meet later in the module - NIST's Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 27001's plan-do-check-act cycle, and OWASP's application-level guidance all follow roughly this same identify → assess → mitigate → monitor shape.

Worked example: the login page

Let's run one realistic scenario through every stage, start to finish.

A
ASSET

Customer credentials and the funds behind them, on an online banking login page.

V

The login form has no limit on failed attempts.

Category: implementation flaw - nothing stops a script from trying thousands of passwords a minute.

T
THREAT

A financially motivated attacker running automated credential-stuffing tools.

Capability: yes (freely available tools). Intent: yes (financial gain). Opportunity: yes (the form is public).

A
ATTACK

A brute-force / credential-stuffing attack against the login endpoint - an active attack aimed at confidentiality and, indirectly, integrity of the account.

I
IMPACT

Account takeover, stolen funds, breach notification costs, reputational damage.

C
COUNTERMEASURES

Rate limiting, account lockout, CAPTCHA and MFA (preventive) · login-attempt monitoring and alerts (detective) · forced password resets after a suspected breach (corrective).

Notice this is exactly the chain from Section 6, walked end-to-end on a real system - and the countermeasures at the bottom are deliberately spread across all three links, not stacked on just one.
10

Quick self-test

Try to answer before you open each one.

Q1. A company's firewall is misconfigured to allow all inbound traffic. Is this a threat or a vulnerability?

A vulnerability. It's a weakness in the defenses. The threat would be whoever might try to use that open door - a scanning bot, an opportunistic attacker, and so on.

Q2. Give one example each of a preventive and a detective countermeasure for the same vulnerability: an unpatched server.

Preventive: apply the patch (removes the vulnerability entirely). Detective: run vulnerability scans / intrusion detection that flags exploitation attempts against that server while it remains unpatched.

Q3. Why is a vulnerability with no known threat still worth tracking?

Because threats change constantly - new attacker groups, new automated tools, new motivations appear all the time. A vulnerability that's "safe" today because nobody is targeting it can become high-risk overnight once a threat actor notices it.

Q4. Sort this into passive or active: an attacker silently copies encrypted traffic to analyse later, without altering anything in transit.

Passive - nothing in the system is changed, only observed/copied. It's precisely why encryption is the standard countermeasure: it can't stop the copying, but it can make the copy useless.

Q5. Two systems have identical vulnerabilities. System A holds public marketing brochures; System B holds medical records. Do they carry the same risk?

No. Threat and vulnerability may be identical, but impact differs hugely, so risk differs hugely too - this is exactly why risk always needs all three inputs, not just "is there a hole."

11

Recap & glossary

Take this away

  • A vulnerability is a weakness; a threat is a potential danger; an attack is that danger acted out against that weakness; a countermeasure pushes back at any of those three stages.
  • Risk needs all three inputs - threat, vulnerability, and impact - not just one.
  • Break the chain at any link and the incident doesn't happen - layer countermeasures across all links, don't bet on one.
  • Assurance isn't a one-off audit - it's a loop: identify, assess, mitigate, monitor, repeat.

Glossary

Asset
Anything of value worth protecting - data, systems, people, reputation.
Vulnerability
A weakness in design, implementation, configuration, process, or people that could be exploited.
Threat
A potential source of harm - an agent with the capability, intent and opportunity to exploit a vulnerability.
Threat agent / actor
The specific person, group, or force behind a threat.
Attack
A deliberate attempt to exploit a vulnerability and violate confidentiality, integrity, or availability.
Attack vector
The specific path used to carry out one attack.
Attack surface
The complete set of points an attacker could potentially use to get in.
Countermeasure / control
An action, device, procedure or technique that reduces a vulnerability, blocks a threat, or limits an attack's impact.
Risk
The combination of likelihood (threat × vulnerability) and impact - the quantity that drives prioritisation.
Assurance
Confidence, built through continuous identify-assess-mitigate-monitor cycles, that a system meets its security requirements.
