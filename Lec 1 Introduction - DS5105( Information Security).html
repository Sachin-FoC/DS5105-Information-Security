Definitions — Read First

What is information security?

Information security — often shortened to infosec — is the practice of protecting information, and the systems that store, process, and move it, from anyone or anything that shouldn't have access to it.

That's a broader mandate than it sounds. It covers a login password scrawled on a sticky note as much as it covers a nation-state intercepting undersea fiber-optic cables. It covers a hospital's patient records, a bank's transaction ledger, a factory's control systems, and the phone in your pocket. If it holds information that matters, it's in scope.

Infosec is frequently confused with — and overlaps heavily with — cybersecurity. The distinction that matters: cybersecurity is specifically about digital systems and networks; information security is the wider discipline, digital and physical, that cybersecurity sits inside. A locked filing cabinet is an information-security control. It just isn't a cybersecurity one.

Nearly everything the field does traces back to three properties every piece of information should have. That's the subject of the next log.

Core Doctrine

The CIA triad

Three properties, one model, used everywhere in the field — from a five-minute security briefing to a formal risk assessment. Every control, every policy, and every incident in this log ultimately maps back to one (or more) of these three.

Confidentiality

Only the people who are supposed to see something can see it. Broken by a leaked database, a shoulder-surfed password, or an unencrypted laptop left in a taxi.

ENFORCED BY: encryption, access control, need-to-know

Integrity

Information stays accurate and unaltered unless someone with the right to change it does so. Broken by a tampered invoice, a corrupted backup, or a hacked vote tally.

ENFORCED BY: checksums, digital signatures, audit trails

Availability

Information and systems are there when the people who legitimately need them try to use them. Broken by a ransomware lockout, a power outage, or a denial-of-service flood.

ENFORCED BY: backups, redundancy, capacity planning
Animated · how the three overlap

Most real incidents violate more than one leg of the triad at once. A ransomware attack, for instance, is primarily an availability attack — but the attackers usually steal a copy of the data first, turning it into a confidentiality breach too.

Threat Intelligence

Who's actually on the other end

"Hacker" is a stand-in for a wide range of very different people with very different budgets, patience, and goals. Knowing which one you're up against changes what "good enough" security looks like.

SK

Script kiddies

Low skill, off-the-shelf tools, opportunistic. They scan the whole internet for anyone still running an unpatched, well-known vulnerability — not you specifically, just anyone.

HA

Hacktivists

Politically or ideologically motivated. Goal is usually disruption or embarrassment — a defaced website, a leaked internal memo — rather than direct financial gain.

OC

Organized cybercrime

Financially motivated, well-resourced, and often run like a business — some ransomware gangs operate customer-support chat for their victims. Highest-volume threat most organizations face.

NS

Nation-state actors

Patient, well-funded, and willing to spend months inside a network before doing anything. Goals range from espionage to pre-positioning for sabotage during a future conflict.

IN

Insiders

Not always malicious — a surprising share of breaches trace back to an employee who was careless, phished, or simply never had their access revoked after leaving. The insider doesn't need to break in; they're already inside.

Attack Techniques

How they actually get in

Most attacks fall into a small set of recurring patterns. Learn these six and you'll recognize the shape of almost everything in the news.

Animated · a typical intrusion path
  • Phishing

    A message impersonating someone trustworthy, engineered to get you to click a link, hand over a password, or open a file. Still the single most common way attackers get their first foothold — because it targets people, not code.

  • Malware

    Software written to do something harmful once it's running on your system: steal data, spy on you, or hand an attacker remote control. Ransomware is malware that encrypts your files and demands payment for the key.

  • Denial of Service

    Flooding a system with more traffic or requests than it can handle, so legitimate users can't get through. A "distributed" denial of service (DDoS) does this from thousands of hijacked devices at once.

  • Man-in-the-Middle

    Secretly sitting between two parties who think they're talking directly to each other — over unsecured Wi-Fi, for instance — reading or altering the traffic as it passes through.

  • SQL injection

    Slipping database commands into a web form the developer expected to only ever contain ordinary text, tricking the application into running them — sometimes handing over an entire database.

  • Social engineering

    The umbrella term for manipulating people rather than machines — a fake IT-support call, a stranger in a uniform tailgating through a locked door. Phishing is just the digital version of this much older technique.

Case Studies

Three incidents that changed the field

Abstract principles get concrete fast once you attach them to a real incident. These three are still cited constantly, for different reasons.

Stuxnet · 2010

A worm that physically damaged centrifuges at an Iranian nuclear facility by subtly altering their spin speed while showing operators normal readings. Widely seen as the moment cyberattacks proved they could cause physical, real-world damage.

WannaCry · 2017

A ransomware worm that spread on its own across networks in over a hundred countries in a matter of hours, exploiting a Windows flaw that already had a patch available. A textbook case for why patching promptly matters.

Target breach · 2013

Attackers got in through credentials stolen from a small third-party HVAC vendor, then moved sideways across the network to reach point-of-sale systems. A lasting lesson in supply-chain risk and network segmentation.

Standard Countermeasures

What actually stops most of this

None of these are exotic. Almost every major breach traces back to one of these basics being missing, misconfigured, or ignored — not to some novel, unstoppable technique.

  • Encryption

    Scrambles data so it's unreadable without the right key — whether it's sitting on a disk or traveling across a network. Turns a stolen laptop into a brick instead of a breach.

  • Multi-factor auth

    Requires a second proof of identity beyond a password — a code, a key, a fingerprint. Neutralizes the vast majority of stolen-password attacks outright.

  • Patch management

    Applying security updates promptly. Most exploited vulnerabilities already had a fix available — the gap was in applying it, not in the vendor's response time.

  • Network segmentation

    Dividing a network into isolated zones so a breach in one area — a guest Wi-Fi network, say — can't automatically reach the systems that actually matter.

  • Backups

    Regular, tested, and — critically — kept offline or otherwise out of reach of anything that could encrypt or delete the live copy along with the backup.

  • Security awareness

    Training people to recognize phishing and social engineering. The strongest firewall in the world doesn't stop someone from typing their password into a convincing fake login page.

Doctrine

Guiding Principles

The rules everything else follows

Individual defenses come and go as technology changes. These principles have held up for decades because they don't depend on any particular tool.

Defense in depth

Layer multiple, independent controls so that one failure doesn't mean total compromise — like a building with a fence, a lock, an alarm, and a safe, not just one of those.

Least privilege

Give every person and system exactly the access they need to do their job, and nothing more. Limits how far an attacker — or a mistake — can spread once inside.

Zero trust

"Never trust, always verify." Don't automatically trust a request just because it came from inside the corporate network — verify every user and device, every time.

Need-to-know

A close cousin of least privilege, applied to information specifically: access to sensitive data is granted only to those who genuinely need it to do their work.

For Individuals, Not Just Organizations

What this means for your own accounts

Everything above scales down. Here's the individual-level version of the same defenses, in order of impact.

  • 01 — Passwords

    Use a unique, long password for every account, generated and stored by a password manager. Reused passwords are the single biggest reason one small breach turns into many.

  • 02 — MFA

    Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered, especially email — it's usually the account attackers use to reset everything else.

  • 03 — Updates

    Install software and OS updates promptly instead of postponing them indefinitely. Most are patching a specific, known hole.

  • 04 — Skepticism

    Treat unexpected urgency ("your account will be suspended in 24 hours") as a red flag, not a reason to act fast. Verify through a separate, known channel before clicking or paying anything.

  • 05 — Backups

    Keep an independent backup of anything you couldn't stand to lose — a second copy that ransomware on your main device can't also reach.

Open Roles

Careers in the Field

Where people in this field actually work

Infosec isn't one job — it's an entire ecosystem of roles, from hands-on-keyboard technical work to policy and governance.

SOC analyst

Watches security alerts in real time and investigates the ones that look real. Usually the entry point into the field.

Penetration tester

Attacks systems on purpose, with permission, to find the holes before someone without permission does.

Security engineer

Designs and builds the actual defenses — firewalls, identity systems, secure infrastructure — that everyone else relies on.

Incident responder

Gets called in once something has already gone wrong, to contain the damage and figure out exactly what happened.

GRC analyst

Governance, risk, and compliance — makes sure the organization actually meets the security standards it's supposed to.

CISO

Chief Information Security Officer — owns the whole security program and answers for it at the executive level.

Closing Statement

This log is never really closed

Information security isn't a state you reach — it's a discipline you keep practicing, because the people on the other side keep adapting too. Nothing in this briefing is a finish line.

If you want to keep going, three places are worth bookmarking: the OWASP project for web-application security fundamentals, NIST's Cybersecurity Framework for how organizations structure a security program, and a foundational certification like CompTIA Security+ if you want a structured path into the field.
