0x01 Framing

What are we actually protecting?

"Security" is not one property — it's a bundle of guarantees a system makes about information and access. Computer & network security exists because Alice and Bob are not alone on the wire: Trudy is always a possible participant. Everything below defines which promise is broken when Trudy succeeds.

AssetAnything of value: data, a session, a credential, compute, bandwidth.
ThreatA potential cause of an unwanted incident (e.g. Trudy on the wire).
ControlA safeguard that reduces risk: encryption, hashing, redundancy, policy.

0x03 Beyond CIA

Accountability, Non-repudiation & Authenticity

CIA doesn't answer "who did this, and can they deny it?" These three properties close that gap — they're what let a security incident end in a courtroom instead of a shrug. Click a card to expand.

01＋

Authenticity

The guarantee that data, a message, or an identity really is what/who it claims to be.

Definition: Authenticity verifies the origin of information — that a message from "Alice" was actually created by Alice, and a server claiming to be "bob-bank.com" really is.

Scenario: Trudy sends Bob an email that looks like it's from Alice ("Bob, wire the funds to this account"). Without an authenticity check (e.g. a digital signature or a verified certificate), Bob has no way to know Alice never sent it.

Typical controls: digital signatures, MACs, TLS certificates, mutual authentication.

02＋

Non-repudiation

Alice cannot later deny she sent the message; Bob cannot deny he received it.

Definition: Provides undeniable proof of origin (and optionally receipt), so a party can't successfully disown an action after the fact.

Scenario: Alice digitally signs a purchase order to Bob's store. Later she tries to claim "I never ordered that." Because the signature was produced with Alice's private key, Bob (and a third party / judge) can cryptographically prove otherwise.

Typical controls: digital signatures + trusted timestamps, audit logs, certified delivery receipts.

03＋

Accountability

Every action can be traced back to the entity that performed it.

Definition: Accountability = identification + logging + auditing, tying actions to accounts so misuse can be investigated after the fact.

Scenario: Trudy compromises a low-privilege account and deletes a file. Because the system logged who / what / when / from where for every action, the audit trail lets the security team reconstruct exactly what Trudy did — even though she was never caught in the act.

Typical controls: audit logging, SIEM, unique user IDs (no shared logins), tamper-evident logs.

0x04 Access Control Model

Authentication → Authorization → Accounting (AAA)

Before Bob's system trusts anything Alice does, it runs her through this pipeline. Click each stage to see what question it answers and to step through a live login console.

STEP 1

Identification

Alice claims an identity: "I am alice@corp".

Q: who are you claiming to be?
›
STEP 2

Authentication

Alice proves it — password, OTP token, fingerprint (something you know / have / are).

Q: can you prove that?
›
STEP 3

Authorization

The system checks Alice's permissions against the resource she's requesting.

Q: are you allowed to do this?
›
STEP 4

Accounting

The action, timestamp, and outcome are logged — feeding Accountability.

Q: what did you just do?

Note the distinction: authentication asks "who are you, prove it," while authorization asks "now that I know who you are, what are you allowed to touch." Trudy can pass step 2 (e.g. with a stolen password) and still fail step 3 if she lacks the right role.
